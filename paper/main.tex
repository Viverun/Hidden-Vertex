\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Graph autoencoders (GAEs) trained only on background are a natural candidate for model-agnostic
new-physics searches. I tested two of them on the LHC Olympics 2020 R\&D dataset, where a
$W'\to XY\to(q\bar q)(q\bar q)$ dijet resonance is hidden in QCD background. The first is a node-level
GAE that scores per-particle reconstruction error. The second encodes the whole event, reconstructs it
with a set decoder and a Chamfer loss, and offers three scores: reconstruction error, latent
$k$-nearest-neighbour distance and latent Gaussian-mixture likelihood. The decision criterion was fixed
before looking at any test result. Neither model beats simple label-free observables. The node-level model reaches an AUC of
$0.483\pm0.005$ with max-reduction and $0.781$ with mean-reduction; all three event-level scores are
\emph{inverted}, with AUCs between $0.401$ and $0.436$, stable across seeds, nine ablations, and
signal-contaminated training. By contrast, the mean transverse momentum per particle,
$\ptmean=\HT/N$, reaches $0.811$, and $-\tauTO$ of the leading jet reaches $0.801$.
Correlation and binned analyses suggest that the latent-density scores track event complexity: the
signal has fewer, harder particles than QCD. A follow-up with five studies does not support
complexity as the main cause. Matching the multiplicity of the background to the signal raises the
event-level AUCs only to $0.44$--$0.50$, and retraining on a multiplicity-matched background does not
recover performance (AUC $0.40$--$0.42$, against a prediction fixed in advance of at least $0.47$).
Density scores on jet-level features reach $0.69$--$0.76$, and a Wasserstein-normalized autoencoder
(WNAE) on the same features reaches $0.72$ in a pilot and $0.64$ in a longer run; none passes the gate.
The WNAE was run on jet features and not on the graph model, and its longer-run settings were fixed after the
pilot test results had been seen. Complexity is at most a partial explanation. As a contrast, a
weakly supervised CWoLa-style bump hunt asks whether an \mjj window holds an excess, and trains on a pool
into which signal is injected, so it is not an unsupervised method. With the signal mass known, its
oracle window passes the gate from a signal fraction of $0.001$ (AUC $0.835$). A version that does not use the mass
(two grids of \mjj windows, fixed in advance) reaches only about $0.74$ and fails three of four
predictions fixed in advance. The benchmark code is public,
and I suggest \ptmean as a standard reference baseline for unsupervised methods on this dataset.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Searches for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM) at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) are
mostly designed around specific signal hypotheses. Model-agnostic anomaly detection aims to
complement them by flagging events that are atypical with respect to the Standard Model
background, without assuming a signal model. The LHC Olympics 2020 (LHCO)~\cite{Kasieczka:2021xcg}
established a common benchmark for this task, and autoencoders, in which events with large
reconstruction error are treated as anomalous, are among the most widely studied unsupervised
approaches~\cite{Farina:2018fyg,Heimel:2018mkt,Finke:2021sdf,Atkinson:2021nlt}.

Autoencoder scores have a known weakness: they can end up measuring how complex an input is
rather than how unusual it is. For jet images, simple signal jets turn out to be \emph{easier} to
reconstruct than QCD jets~\cite{Finke:2021sdf}, and likelihood-based generative models show related
effects~\cite{Nalisnick:2018dgm,Serra:2019ocd}. This project started as an attempt to build a
working graph-based detector. It produced inverted scores for particle graphs of whole events, and I first
attributed this to complexity. A follow-up did not support that as the main explanation.
This paper documents what happened:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a reproducible benchmark harness on the public LHCO R\&D data, with a frozen test set,
        multi-seed runs, bootstrap confidence intervals and within-bin AUCs that test whether
        a score carries information beyond trivial event quantities;
  \item a set of label-free reference baselines, among which the mean transverse momentum per
        particle, $\ptmean=\HT/N$, is the strongest particle-level observable
        (AUC $0.811$), above \HT, \mjj, and a node-level GAE;
  \item a negative result, under a decision rule fixed in advance, for an event-level GAE whose three anomaly scores are
        systematically inverted, with ablations, a contamination study, and a correlation-based
        analysis that points to event complexity as a possible factor;
  \item a follow-up (Part~3) that tests this: density scores and a Wasserstein-normalized
        autoencoder~\cite{CMS:2025lmn} on jet features, complexity-matched evaluation and retraining, a
        reverse-direction check, and a weakly supervised bump hunt as a contrast. None of the
        unsupervised scores passes the gate, and matching the multiplicity does not recover
        performance. The bump hunt passes only when the signal mass is used.
\end{enumerate}
None of this is a working anomaly detector. The practical point is simpler: before claiming that
an unsupervised model has learned something on this dataset, compare it with \ptmean. If it cannot
beat a ratio of two event totals, it may have learned little beyond the energy and multiplicity scale.
Part~3 shows that this reading needs care: the advantage of \ptmean largely disappears when
the background is matched to the signal in multiplicity and \HT, but the learned scores do not
become useful either.

\section{Dataset and test protocol}
\label{sec:data}

I use the LHCO 2020 R\&D dataset~\cite{Kasieczka:2021xcg,LHCO:zenodo}. Background consists of
QCD dijet events and signal of $W'\to XY$ with $m_{W'}=3.5$~TeV, $m_X=500$~GeV and
$m_Y=100$~GeV, each of $X$ and $Y$ decaying to two quarks. Events were generated with
\textsc{Pythia}~8~\cite{Sjostrand:2014zea} and the \textsc{Delphes}~3~\cite{deFavereau:2013fsa}
detector simulation, and are stored as up to 700 hadrons
$(\pt,\eta,\phi)$. Jet-level features (anti-$k_t$~\cite{Cacciari:2008gp} $R=1$ jet masses,
$N$-subjettiness ratios~\cite{Thaler:2010tr} and \mjj) are taken from the accompanying
feature file.

One practical warning first: the publicly available file \texttt{events\_anomalydetection\_v2.h5} is \emph{sorted}: rows
$0$--$999{,}999$ are background and rows $\geq 1{,}000{,}000$ are signal. Any pipeline that
reads a prefix of the file therefore sees no signal at all; I select events explicitly by row
index. The test set is frozen across all experiments: $5{,}000$ background events
(rows $40{,}000$--$44{,}999$) and $5{,}000$ signal events (rows $1{,}000{,}000$--$1{,}004{,}999$).
Models are trained on $180{,}000$ background-only events (rows $\geq 45{,}000$) with a
$20{,}000$-event background validation set.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
I report the ROC AUC with bootstrap confidence intervals over the $10{,}000$ test events
(95\% for baselines; 98.3\% for the three model scores, a Bonferroni correction for three
comparisons). To test whether a score carries information beyond trivial event quantities,
I also compute the AUC \emph{within bins} of \HT, particle multiplicity $N$, and leading-particle
\pt. The sign of every score (``larger = more anomalous'') is fixed in advance.

\paragraph{Decision criterion.}
Before any test result for the event-level model, I fixed the following gate: a model score
passes only if the lower bound of its seed-averaged AUC interval exceeds the best particle-level
baseline, and its minimum within-bin AUC is at least $0.6$.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{Node-level GAE.}
Each event is a radius graph with edges between particles at $\Delta R<0.4$. A graph
convolutional encoder~\cite{Kipf:2016gcn} compresses each particle's features to a one-dimensional
latent and a decoder reconstructs its standardized $(\pt,\eta,\phi)$; the model has 420 effective
parameters. The event score is the maximum or the mean of the per-particle reconstruction
errors.

\paragraph{Event-level GAE.}
The encoder applies three residual neighbour-averaging layers of width 64 on the same
$\Delta R<0.4$ graph, followed by concatenated mean, max and \pt-weighted pooling into a
16-dimensional event latent. A set decoder reconstructs the $K=48$ hardest particles and is
trained with a symmetric Chamfer distance~\cite{Fan:2017chamfer}, which is invariant to particle
ordering. Events are truncated to the 350 hardest particles, a cut chosen from the measured
multiplicity distribution before training ($0.7\%$ of background and $0.02\%$ of signal events
exceed it). The model has $95{,}888$ parameters and is trained for 30 epochs with AdamW
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 256, early-stopping patience 5) on one NVIDIA T4 GPU,
about 18 minutes per seed. Three anomaly scores are evaluated: (i) the Chamfer reconstruction
error; (ii) the mean distance to the 20 nearest training latents; and (iii) the negative
log-likelihood of the latent under an 8-component Gaussian mixture fitted to training latents.

For the node-level GAE, the choice of reduction matters more than the model: the same trained
network gives $0.48$ with max and $0.78$ with mean. Across five seeds, the max-reduction AUC is
$0.483\pm0.005$. Scaling the network by $44\times$ in parameters lowers the training loss by about

perfectly, the residual error originates in the neighbourhood averaging of the graph convolution.
The mean score retains AUC $0.61$--$0.82$ within deciles of \HT, $N$ and leading \pt, but it is
strongly anti-correlated with multiplicity ($\rho=-0.71$) and does not exceed \ptmean.

\section{Correlation analysis of the Part 2 scores}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

Table~\ref{tab:corr} gives Spearman correlations of the seed-0 scores with simple event
quantities. The latent-density scores increase with multiplicity ($\rho=+0.28$ for $k$NN,
$+0.23$ for GMM) and decrease with \ptmean ($-0.27$, $-0.20$), also within background alone. Their
AUC within multiplicity bins is close to chance ($k$NN $0.43$--$0.50$, GMM $0.47$--$0.51$).
One reading is that signal events have fewer and harder particles than QCD background and so fall
into dense regions of a latent space trained on QCD. Under the pre-declared sign (far from training data
means anomalous), this would produce AUCs below $0.5$. The correlations are consistent with this
reading, but they do not test it. Section~\ref{sec:part3} tests it directly, and the result is that
complexity is at most a partial explanation.

The Chamfer score behaves differently: it barely tracks multiplicity ($\rho=+0.08$) or \ptmean
($\rho=-0.07$), and remains inverted within multiplicity bins ($0.41$--$0.48$) and within \HT
bins (down to $0.31$). Its inversion is therefore not explained by multiplicity alone, and its
origin remains open. The three scores are only moderately correlated with one another (Chamfer--$k$NN
$0.53$, Chamfer--GMM $0.42$, $k$NN--GMM $0.87$). Flipping the sign post hoc would give AUCs of
$0.56$--$0.60$, still far below \ptmean. I mention this only to show that the scores contain a
weak complexity signal rather than noise; it is not a result.

\section{Part 3: follow-up tests}
\label{sec:part3}

The explanation in Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis} rests on correlations. Part~3 asks whether it
survives a direct test, and whether other inputs or a different training objective change the
outcome. It has five studies: (A) density and autoencoder scores on jet-level features;
(B) a Wasserstein-normalized autoencoder (WNAE) on the same features; (C) complexity-matched
evaluation and retraining; (D) a reverse-direction check; and (E) a weakly supervised
CWoLa-style bump hunt, as a contrast to the outlier scores of (A)--(D). The test set, the gate, the score
sign (larger = more anomalous) and the training and validation splits are the same as in
Parts~1 and~2. No model, epoch or score sign was chosen from test AUC. One setting was fixed
after test results had been seen; Section~\ref{sec:disclosures} states it.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

Likelihood-based generative models are known to assign high likelihood to inputs from other
distributions~\cite{Nalisnick:2018dgm}, and Serr\`a et al.\ link the effect to input complexity~\cite{Serra:2019ocd}.
The complexity effect for autoencoders in this paper looks similar, but I did not test the link.
Normalized autoencoders (NAEs) address a related weakness of plain autoencoders: they can
reconstruct inputs far from the training data well. An NAE adds a normalization constraint and
trains with negative samples drawn from the model's own energy~\cite{Yoon:2021nae}; it was
applied to LHC jets by Dillon et al.~\cite{Dillon:2022mkq}. The WNAE of the CMS
Collaboration~\cite{CMS:2025lmn} replaces the NAE objective with a Wasserstein distance between
data and negative samples. Other work discusses topological obstructions to autoencoding~\cite{Batson:2021agz},
bump hunting with a variational autoencoder that has physical observables in its latent space~\cite{Bortolato:2021zic}, and a graph autoencoder
for anomaly detection at the LHC~\cite{Araz:2025oax}. The Part~3 results do not test any of these claims in their own
setting. They test one WNAE-style objective on one signal model.

\subsection{Methods}
\label{sec:part3methods}

\paragraph{Jet-feature scores (A).}
The inputs are the jet masses $m_{j1}$ and $m_{j2}$, the ratios $\tau_{21}$ of the two leading
jets, and \mjj (``jet5''); ``jet4'' is the same set without \mjj. Features are mapped to a
standard normal with a quantile transform fitted on the $180{,}000$ training events. Three scores are
fitted on background only: the mean distance to the 20 nearest training events ($k$NN), the negative
log-likelihood under an 8-component full-covariance Gaussian mixture (GMM), and the reconstruction error
of an MLP autoencoder (5--32--32--3--32--32--5 with GELU, AdamW, 30 epochs, early-stopping
patience 5). Three seeds are used. The $k$NN score does not depend on the seed.

\paragraph{WNAE (B).}
I re-implemented the WNAE following the public reference code of the CMS authors. It was run on
the jet5 features, \emph{not} on the graph model, because it needs a fixed-size input vector. The
network is the MLP autoencoder above, and its energy is the reconstruction MSE. Negative samples
come from 10 steps of Langevin MCMC (step size $0.0242$, noise $0.22$, temperature $0.063$) with a
persistent buffer of $10{,}000$ samples, $5\%$ restarts from a standard normal, and samples
clipped to $[-3,3]$. The loss is the exact Wasserstein-1 distance between a batch of data and a batch of
negatives (network simplex, POT library). Adam is used with learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$. The
saved model is the epoch with the lowest validation Wasserstein distance, which does not use signal
labels. The ground cost, optimiser and learning-rate schedule differ from the reference code, and
the inputs and the data are not those of the CMS study. Two runs are reported. The \emph{pilot} uses
batch size $4096$ and $6$ epochs, fixed from a timing run before any test AUC was computed. The
\emph{long} run uses batch size $1024$ and up to $150$ epochs, with early stopping when the
validation distance had not improved by $0.1\%$ in $30$ epochs. The long-run settings were fixed
\emph{after} the pilot test AUCs had been seen (Section~\ref{sec:disclosures}).

\paragraph{Complexity matching (C).}
Two kinds of matching use the signal's multiplicity and \HT distributions. This uses signal
information, so both are diagnostics and not detectors.
\emph{Matched evaluation}: background test events are weighted so that their distribution
over bins of multiplicity (20 quantile bins), or over $8\times8$ bins of multiplicity and \HT,
equals the signal's. Weights are clipped at the 99th percentile. The 95\% bootstrap intervals
recompute the weights in every resample.
\emph{Matched retraining}: the Part~2 event-level model is trained again on a background sample
drawn with replacement from the training events with weights that make the multiplicity
distribution follow that of $16{,}200$ signal events outside the test set ($30$ quantile bins,
weights clipped at the $99.5$th percentile). The validation set is not reweighted.
The expected outcome was fixed before any result: if multiplicity were the cause of the inversion,
the retrained scores would reach an AUC of at least $0.47$. For matched evaluation I fixed that if
complexity alone explained the $k$NN and GMM inversion, their matched AUC would lie within
$0.5\pm0.03$.

\paragraph{Reverse direction (D).}
As a diagnostic that uses signal labels, the $k$NN, GMM and MLP-autoencoder scores are fitted on
$76{,}000$ \emph{signal} events (autoencoder validation: $9{,}000$ more), and the frozen test set is
scored with the background as the ``anomalous'' class. The quantile transform is refitted on the
signal set. This is not an anomaly-detection result.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:part3results}

\paragraph{Jet-feature scores.}
Table~\ref{tab:jet} lists the seed-averaged results. With \mjj included, the density scores reach
$0.717$ ($k$NN) and $0.694$ (GMM); without \mjj they reach $0.761$ and $0.753$. The density scores are
better than the Part~2 scores, but all are below the bar of $0.811$ and below the best single jet observable
$-\tauTO$ ($0.801$). The MLP autoencoder is close to chance on both feature sets ($0.505$ and
$0.539$). Removing \mjj raises the AUC of the density scores. It also raises their minimum within-bin AUC from
$0.55$--$0.57$ to $0.67$--$0.68$, so the jet4 density scores meet the within-bin requirement of $0.6$.
No jet-feature score exceeds the bar, so \textbf{the gate is not passed}. The maximum
significance improvement (TPR$/\sqrt{\mathrm{FPR}}$ at FPR $\geq10^{-3}$) of the density scores is $1.1$--$1.3$,
against $2.28$ for the oracle mass window.

\subsection{Weak-supervision contrast: a CWoLa-style bump hunt}
\label{sec:cwola}

The scores above ask whether an event is rare under the background. A bump hunt asks a different
question: is there an excess of events in some \mjj window? This study is a contrast and is
\emph{not} an unsupervised method trained on pure background. Signal is injected into the unlabeled
training pool, so the setting is weakly supervised, and it assumes that the new physics appears as a
resonance in \mjj. I use the idea of classification without labels (CWoLa)~\cite{Metodiev:2017vrx,Collins:2018epr}:
a classifier trained to separate events in a signal region (SR) of \mjj from events in neighbouring
sidebands (SB) learns the features in which the two regions differ, and an excess of signal in the SR
makes those features useful for finding the signal. The protocol was fixed on 14 November 2026, before any score
was computed on the test set (\texttt{configs/part3\_cwola.yaml}), and test AUC selects nothing.

\paragraph{Method.}
The unlabeled pool holds the $955{,}000$ background rows outside the frozen test set (rows $\geq45{,}000$)
plus injected signal events from rows $\geq1{,}005{,}000$, which are never in the test set. The
signal-to-background ratio $S/B$ in the pool is $0$, $0.0005$, $0.001$, $0.002$, $0.005$ or $0.01$
(for $0.001$: $955$ signal events). Three seeds choose the injected events and all splits.
The pool is split $70\%/30\%$. The first part trains the classifiers; the second is a reference sample
that only supplies the score distribution of each window. The classifiers are boosted trees
(scikit-learn \texttt{HistGradientBoostingClassifier}, 200 iterations at most, early stopping,
balanced class weights), as an ensemble of 10 models, and they use the jet4 features ($m_{j1}$, $m_{j2}$ and
$\tau_{21}$ of both jets). \mjj is \emph{not} an input; it only defines the windows.
Two grids of windows of width $0.4$~TeV, offset by half a window, cover $2.0$--$5.2$~TeV (grid A) and
$2.2$--$5.4$~TeV (grid B). The edges are round numbers and are not centred on the signal mass of $3.5$~TeV.
Each SR has a sideband of $0.2$~TeV on each side, and the outer windows are open-ended with one sideband of
$0.4$~TeV. A test event is assigned to the window of each grid that holds its \mjj. Its score is the fraction of
reference-sample SR events of that window with a lower ensemble output (a quantile), averaged over the two
grids. A larger value means more anomalous. No window needed merging in any run.
The test set, the gate and the bar of $0.811$ are those of the other studies, and the gate is judged at
$S/B=0.001$, the level of the LHCO benchmark. Confidence intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps
of the seed-mean AUC.

\paragraph{Oracle window.}
For reference only, the same classifier and features are trained for one window that uses the known signal mass:
SR $3.3$--$3.7$~TeV with sidebands $3.1$--$3.3$ and $3.7$--$3.9$~TeV. Its score is the raw ensemble output
for every test event. This window needs knowledge of the signal and is not a model-independent result.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:cwola} gives the AUCs. Without injected signal the score is at $0.532$, and the
AUC of the window-agnostic score rises to $0.716$ at $S/B=0.001$ and then stays at $0.735$--$0.741$ from
$S/B=0.002$ upward. At $S/B=0.001$ the lower bound of the interval is $0.707$, so the gate is not passed.
The minimum within-bin AUC is $0.628$ there and does not stop it; the AUC is the limit. The oracle window
reaches $0.835$ at $S/B=0.001$ (interval $0.828$--$0.842$, minimum within-bin AUC $0.733$) and rises to $0.928$
at $S/B=0.01$. It passes the gate from $S/B=0.001$ upward. These gate decisions for the oracle are for
information only, because the oracle uses the signal mass.

\paragraph{Predictions fixed in advance.}
Four predictions were fixed before any test score was computed. \emph{Three failed and one passed.}
(1)~At $S/B=0$ the seed-mean AUC should lie in $[0.47,0.53]$: it is $0.532$, just outside, so this
\textbf{failed}. The two grids differ ($0.555$ for grid A, $0.492$ for grid B), and I did not establish
the cause. (2)~The seed-mean AUC should not decrease as $S/B$ grows (tolerance $0.01$): the largest
decrease is $0.006$ (from $0.002$ to $0.005$), so this \textbf{passed}. (3)~At $S/B=0.001$ the gate should be passed:
the lower bound is $0.707$, so this \textbf{failed}. (4)~At $S/B\geq0.005$ the AUC should be above $0.90$:
it is $0.735$ and $0.739$, so this \textbf{failed}.

\paragraph{Possible explanation of the plateau (hypothesis, not tested here).}
The window-agnostic score stops improving near $0.74$ while the oracle window keeps improving. One possible
cause is that the signal peak lies near a window edge. Signal events that are close to the peak but fall
in a neighbouring window put signal into the sidebands of the window that holds the peak, and
the classifier of that window is then pushed to score signal low. The pool counts support only that
the signal is present in both grids near $3.5$~TeV; I did not test the explanation, for example by
shifting the grids or by removing the signal from the sidebands. I did \emph{not} change the window design
after seeing these results. A better mass-agnostic design is left to future work.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
These numbers show that an excess-based method with the signal mass known clears the bar of $0.811$ at the
benchmark signal fraction, while the outlier scores of this paper did not. They do not show that a
bump hunt in general beats outlier scores: the mass-agnostic version tested here, with one window design
and one signal model, did not clear the bar, and the result for the oracle window depends on the
signal mass and on the injected signal. The two settings also differ in what they assume (a resonance in \mjj,
and signal in the training pool), so the AUCs are not a like-for-like comparison.

\subsection{What was and was not done}
\label{sec:disclosures}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{WNAE settings fixed after seeing test AUC.} The pilot settings were fixed before
        test AUCs were computed. The long-run settings (batch size $1024$, up to $150$ epochs, early-stopping
        patience $30$) were fixed \emph{after} the pilot test AUCs had been seen. The stated reasons are
        convergence (the pilot's best epoch was its last) and compute cost, but I cannot exclude that
        the pilot result influenced the choice. Both runs are reported, and neither is selected.
  \item \textbf{WNAE was run on jet features, not on the graph model.} It needs a fixed-size input.
        Its result says nothing about a WNAE of the particle graph, and it is a re-implementation, not the
        CMS code or configuration.
  \item \textbf{No intervals for the complexity-matched comparison.} The (multiplicity, \HT)
        matching leaves a background effective sample size of about $830$. Table~\ref{tab:matchcmp}
        gives point estimates only.
  \item \textbf{The multiplicity-matched retraining set had about $48\%$ duplicate events} (about
        $92{,}900$ unique events in $180{,}000$ draws) and matched multiplicity only, not \HT.
  \item \textbf{Matching and the reverse check use signal information.} They are diagnostics.
  \item \textbf{Seeds.} Three seeds per model. The pilot WNAE seeds differ by up to $0.1$ in AUC.
  \item \textbf{The bump hunt uses injected signal in training.} Signal events are added to the unlabeled
        pool (Section~\ref{sec:cwola}), so it is a weakly supervised setting and not an unsupervised one. It
        also assumes a resonance in \mjj.
  \item \textbf{The oracle window uses the known signal mass.} It is shown for reference only, and its gate
        decisions are not a model-independent result.
  \item \textbf{The zero-injection AUC of $0.532$ is slightly above chance, and its cause is not established.}
        It lies just outside the interval fixed in advance, $[0.47,0.53]$, and the two window grids
        differ ($0.555$ and $0.492$).
  \item \textbf{The plateau explanation of the bump hunt is a hypothesis.} It was not tested, and the window design
        was not changed after the results were seen.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and outlook}
\label{sec:discussion}

These results are consistent with earlier reports that autoencoder-based anomaly scores can
depend on input complexity~\cite{Finke:2021sdf}, but they do not show that complexity is the cause of the
failures here. Matching the multiplicity moves the event-level scores closer to $0.5$
(for example $k$NN from $0.401$ to $0.461$), yet retraining on a multiplicity-matched background
leaves the AUCs at $0.40$--$0.42$, and no matched score is informative. Complexity is at most a
partial explanation. The remaining deficit is not explained: candidates I have not tested include the
choice of input representation, the pooling and latent space of the graph encoder, and the
topological limits of a low-dimensional latent~\cite{Batson:2021agz}. The
normalized-autoencoder approach was designed against autoencoders that reconstruct unfamiliar inputs
well~\cite{Yoon:2021nae,Dillon:2022mkq,CMS:2025lmn}. In this work it did not pass the gate on jet
features (AUC $0.64$--$0.72$), and it was not tried on the graph model, so its fit to this problem
is open. Two practical lessons follow. First, unsupervised methods on the LHCO R\&D data
should be compared not only with \HT but with \ptmean, which already reaches AUC $0.81$ without
any training. Second, within-bin AUCs at fixed multiplicity and \HT are a cheap and informative
diagnostic: a score that loses its discrimination at fixed complexity has not learned
substructure. A matched evaluation, as in Section~\ref{sec:part3}, is a third check. In that test the
advantage of \ptmean over the jet-feature scores vanishes, while $-\tauTO$ keeps its AUC of $0.805$.
The bump hunt of Section~\ref{sec:cwola} gives a different contrast. With the signal mass known, an
excess-based score clears the bar that the outlier scores failed (AUC $0.835$ at $S/B=0.001$), but the
version without the mass did not (AUC $0.716$, and about $0.74$ at larger $S/B$), and it needs signal in the training pool. A
better mass-agnostic window design is left to future work.

The features that distinguish the signal in this dataset are jet-level: two-prong
substructure (\tauTO), jet masses and \mjj. These are not available to the encoder as explicit
inputs. Giving them to density models and to a WNAE (Part~3) helped
($0.69$--$0.76$ against $0.40$--$0.44$), but did not pass the gate. Natural next steps are therefore (i) a graph-level
WNAE or other normalized autoencoder trained with jet-level and substructure inputs,
(ii) conditioning the model on, or regressing out, multiplicity and \ptmean so that the score
measures structure at fixed complexity, and (iii) choosing the score sign on background-only or
simulated data. Large foundation models for collider physics trained on simulated events are
expected to face the same issue. Benchmarks of the kind released here can be used to check
whether their anomaly scores capture more than event complexity.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All results use a single signal model and the R\&D dataset, not the LHCO black boxes. The
event-level model was evaluated in one configuration fixed in advance with limited ablations.
The complexity explanation is correlational, and the Part~3 tests did not support it as the main
cause; they cannot rule out a smaller contribution. Ablation and contamination runs use two or
one seeds, so differences below $\sim0.02$ should not be interpreted. The WNAE is my
re-implementation on five jet features, with a pilot and a longer run that disagree
(Section~\ref{sec:disclosures}); it is not a test of the CMS method. The complexity-matched
comparison has no confidence intervals because of a small effective sample size, and the
multiplicity-matched retraining set contained about $48\%$ duplicate events. The bump hunt uses one
window design, injected signal and a single signal model, and its oracle window uses the signal mass.
